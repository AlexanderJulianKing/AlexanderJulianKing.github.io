Project Soothsayer

Predicting how people will rate a language model on Arena, from benchmark data alone

I built Soothsayer to predict a model’s Arena score before anyone votes on it. It combines four benchmarks I designed with 11 public leaderboards, fills the gaps in that data with a per-cell imputer, and predicts each model’s Arena score to within about 0.8%. Because the predictions hold up, I can also use the model to see which measurable capabilities move human preference, a question that benchmarks alone cannot answer.

(1)

Why this exists

i

The problem

Arena ranks models by millions of blind head-to-head votes, so its scores are a measure of what people prefer. That is a different quantity from intelligence or objective quality, and it is hard to pin down. Traditional benchmarks do not measure it, and it is not clear that they can.

Why it is hard

Benchmarks are tests of well-defined capabilities such as coding, mathematics, and logical reasoning. Human preference is closer to a black box, and the model that scores well is not always the model people prefer. The data are also hard to model: 138 models with Arena scores, 122 features, about 40% of the benchmark matrix missing, and a noisy target from human votes. The problem is high-dimensional and low-sample at the same time.

The big picture

ii

I combine my benchmarks and the public ones into one dataset, fill its gaps by imputation, and predict Arena scores with an adaptive nearest-neighbor model.

Four benchmarks of my own

iv

I designed each one to cover something the public benchmarks miss.

TrueSkill arena

Soothsayer EQ

Emotional intelligence: empathy, social nuance, and conflict resolution. Models compete head to head, and an LLM referee judges each match.

TrueSkill arena

Soothsayer Writing

A pairwise creative-writing tournament. Stories are judged on character authenticity, originality, coherence, and atmosphere, with penalties for clichés, flowery verbosity, and gratuitous metaphor.

LLM grading

Soothsayer Logic

Twelve physics and lateral-thinking puzzles on commonsense reasoning, graded by an LLM judge.

Style analysis

Soothsayer Style

A profile of each model’s writing: vocabulary richness, sentence patterns, and tone, plus a 32-dimensional semantic embedding of its raw responses (bge-small, then PCA). These are stylistic qualities that people respond to and that benchmark scores do not include.

(5)

The ModelBankImputer

v

About 40% of the public benchmark cells are missing, spread across 65+ highly collinear features. I impute each cell separately, using that correlation structure to pick the most informative predictors for each gap instead of applying one global strategy.

(6)

From features to predictions

vi

An adaptive nearest-neighbor model, because what people prefer changes with how capable a model already is.

The key finding

The same benchmark can correlate with Arena scores in opposite directions depending on how capable the models are. List formatting, for example, correlates positively with human preference among mid-tier models (ρ = +0.16) and negatively among top-tier models (ρ = −0.74). If I fit one global model, these two effects nearly cancel, so I fit a model that adapts to the local regime instead.

  1. Fold-internal PLS-3 hybrid

    In a plain Euclidean distance over ~119 benchmark columns, every column counts equally, so two models can look like neighbors just because they agree on features that do not move the Arena score. Before the nearest-neighbor step, I fit three supervised summary axes inside the current cross-validation fold and append them to the feature matrix. Each axis is a weighted combination of raw columns chosen to track the Arena score globally, so three dimensions of the distance are aligned with the target. The raw columns remain in step 3’s local ridge regression, so the tier-specific sign flips described above are still handled neighborhood by neighborhood. Fitting PLS inside each fold, on training data only, keeps the target from leaking into held-out rows.

  2. Adaptive KNN with a sublinear power cutoff

    For each model, I find its nearest neighbors in the augmented feature space: ~119 raw columns plus the 3 PLS axes, after dropping the style and tone columns, since accuracy was lower with them once the supervised axes were in. The neighborhood radius is a sublinear power law, max_dist = dnearest0.7 × 3. Neighborhoods are tighter in dense regions (top models, k ≈ 25) and still wide enough in sparse regions, so each local regression stays within one consistent feature-preference regime.

  3. Kernel-weighted ridge regression

    Within each neighborhood, I fit a weighted ridge regression in which closer neighbors carry more weight (a Gaussian kernel). The regularization strength is set by the spread of scores in the neighborhood.

  4. Jackknife bias correction

    In ridge regression, predictions are shrunk toward the neighborhood mean. To measure how much, I leave each neighbor out in turn, refit, and compare. The resulting compression slope, typically 1.1 to 1.5, is used to stretch the prediction back out and correct that centering bias.

Adaptive neighborhoods

The cutoff radius is dnearest0.7 × 3, sublinear in the nearest-neighbor distance. Distinctive models, with few close peers in feature space, get a small d0 and a tight neighborhood. Models that cluster together share a small d0 too, but many peers fall within that tight radius, so k is large. Each neighborhood becomes its own training set for a kernel-weighted ridge regression, with different dominant features depending on the regime.

How well it works

vii

Why not just fill the gaps with the median?

Chronological holdout

A stricter test: I train on the oldest 80% of models and predict the newest 20% (n = 22) one model at a time, refitting imputation, PCA, and PLS at every step, so no information from later models is used. The simple baselines get much worse when they have to extrapolate past their training data. The public-only baseline goes from 26.5 to 33.5 RMSE and from R² 0.79 to 0.53. The full pipeline stays at 12.2 RMSE on these newest models.

Calibrated uncertainty

Every prediction comes with a conformal 95% interval. Out of fold, 92.8% of those intervals contain the true score, close to the nominal 95%.

Reproducible and tested

Every number on this page can be regenerated from a clean clone with one command, git clone && ./predict.sh, which prints RMSE 13.90, because the data snapshot is committed with the code. The imputation, scoring, and arena logic are covered by a 130-test suite.
